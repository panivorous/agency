I'd like you to hand work to subagents whenever it pays, without waiting for me to ask. Claude Code's own guidance says not to start them unless I ask; this rule is my asking. A subagent starts cold, has to be briefed, and its result has to be checked, so it pays when the work handed over clearly outweighs that overhead, when its output would clutter your context (a large search, long logs), when independent pieces of work can run at the same time, or when a second opinion is worth having.

Each subagent has one of two roles, and the model it gets follows from the role.

A subordinate does work whose aim and scope you have settled: the reasoning and the decisions stay with you, and it does the labour. It runs on Sonnet. Work that needs judgement that Sonnet may not manage isn't subordinate work; keep that judgement yourself and hand over only the parts that don't need it. Treat what a subordinate reports as evidence to check, not established fact: work that looks mechanical often hides edge cases, and a subordinate that guesses and reports success costs more than it saves.

A peer gives a fresh view of your work. It runs on Opus whatever model you are on: a peer weaker than Opus could not meaningfully challenge your work, and I can't afford a stronger one. Its value is independence: it catches the mistakes that come from your path through the problem, such as anchoring, sunk cost or a cluttered context, and those are hard to notice from inside. So don't wait until you feel unsure. Consult one before committing to a design that later work builds on; before acting on a diagnosis that rests on a single hypothesis you can't cheaply test; after a second failed attempt at the same fix; and before a step that is hard to undo, costly if wrong and resting on your judgement alone. Its view is advice. The decision stays yours; where it is mine, the peer's view goes into the background you give me.

Use the general-purpose agent for either role. A built-in agent made for one job, such as claude-code-guide for Claude Code's documentation, is fine for that job. Pass `model` on every Agent call, `sonnet` for a subordinate and `opus` for a peer, so the model is always a deliberate choice rather than whatever the defaults resolve to. For a subordinate, a fork suits work that genuinely depends on our conversation, not merely work that would be tedious to brief. A fork always runs on your own model, whatever is passed: the one case where a subordinate isn't on Sonnet.

The general-purpose agent gets neither Claude Code's main system prompt nor our conversation, only its own built-in prompt and my CLAUDE.md and rules. So the brief carries everything else it needs: the task and its purpose, what done looks like, and the name of any skill it should load, since it finds skills only through their descriptions and won't look for one it doesn't know it needs. A subordinate's brief also tells it to stay within the task, to settle minor ambiguities itself and say how, to hand back any ambiguity whose answer would change the task, and to say what it checked. Subordinates running at the same time share your working tree: give them separate files, and tell each what the others are changing, so that none touches the others' unfinished work or mistakes it for a failure of its own.

Start a peer fresh rather than as a fork, since a fork inherits the path the peer is there to check. Its brief gives it my request and the decisions I have settled, both in my own words, along with the evidence and whatever it is to judge, such as a design or a step you propose. It leaves out your own verdict on that, which would anchor the peer. The brief asks for an overall verdict as well as any specific checks, invites the peer to question the brief itself, and tells it to leave your files unchanged. If it needs to experiment, start it with `isolation: "worktree"`. That gives it a worktree of its own, a checkout of your current commit (my settings set `worktree.baseRef` to `head`) without your uncommitted changes or ignored files, so the brief says how to bring across what it needs, such as a patch in your scratchpad. While a peer runs, carry on with other work, not with what it is judging. If you go back to a peer, reply with reasons and evidence, not with the outcome you want.

Where I or a skill have already specified the subagents, follow that; this rule fills in only what is left open. A subagent doesn't start subagents of its own unless its brief asks it to. When it does start them, this rule applies with its caller in my place. Otherwise, a subagent doing the work it was given can ignore this rule.
